\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
% Paragraph 1: setting and motivation.
% ------------------------------------------------------------------
Prepare-and-measure protocols certify secrecy from the structure of the
observed statistics: Alice prepares one of a set of states labeled
$x$, Bob measures with a setting $y$ and obtains an outcome $b$, and the
pair distills a shared secret from the correlations $P(b|x,y)$ together
with the operational equivalences that the preparations and measurements
are promised to satisfy. When the statistics witness preparation
contextuality~\cite{Spekkens2005}, no eavesdropper whose side information
is mediated by a noncontextual (classical) model can predict the
outcomes perfectly, and a positive secret-key rate can survive.
\todo{Expand motivation; cite contextuality-based QKD and randomness
literature.}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
% Paragraph 2: the security paradigm considered in this work.
% ------------------------------------------------------------------
In this work we analyze security against \emph{individual
entangling-probe attacks with delayed, setting-dependent product
measurements}. In each round, the eavesdropper Eve attaches an ancillary
probe to the system in transit and lets it interact, but she does not
measure immediately: she waits until the classical announcement of the
measurement setting $y$ (and, where relevant, of which rounds are
key-generating), and only then measures her probe, with a measurement
that may depend arbitrarily on $y$. Crucially, the attack is
\emph{individual}: Eve's probe interacts with each round separately and
is measured with a product (round-by-round) strategy, rather than a
collective or coherent attack across rounds. Within this paradigm we
consider two classes of adversary, matched to the two solver backends of
our accompanying code. The \emph{operational adversary} is constrained
only by the operational theory reproducing the observed statistics: her
side information is an arbitrary decomposition of the observed behavior
into subnormalized behaviors consistent with the promised operational
equivalences. Bounding this adversary's guessing probability is a linear
program (LP). The \emph{quantum adversary} is additionally constrained
by quantum theory: the joint state of message and probe, and Eve's
setting-dependent probe measurements, must admit a quantum realization.
Bounding this adversary is a noncommutative polynomial optimization
problem, which we relax to a semidefinite program (SDP) in the spirit of
the Navascu\'es--Pironio--Ac\'in hierarchy~\cite{NPA2008}, handling
nonprojective measurements through an explicit Naimark-unitary
construction. \todo{Sharpen the formal definitions and point to the
technical sections.}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
% Paragraph 3: from protocols to key rates (the pipeline).
% ------------------------------------------------------------------
Our second contribution is a general-purpose, open-source pipeline that
converts a protocol specification into certified key rates. A protocol
is specified as a contextuality scenario: a behavior $P(b|x,y)$ (given
numerically, or generated from quantum states and effects, or from
states and effects of a generalized probabilistic theory) together with
its preparation and measurement operational equivalences, which the code
can also discover automatically. From this data the pipeline computes,
for each measurement setting $y$, Eve's optimal guessing probability
$\pguess(\cdot\,|y)$ for the raw key variable---held by Alice or by Bob,
as configured---under either adversary model, and converts these
guessing probabilities into uncertainties (min-entropy, or the tighter
reverse-Fano bound) and thence into asymptotic secret-key rates by
subtracting the error-correction cost between the honest parties.
Because every optimization is solved together with its dual, the
pipeline additionally returns human-readable \emph{witness
inequalities}: linear functionals of the observed behavior that certify
the reported bound and expose exactly which features of the statistics
power the security claim. \todo{Forward-reference the sections deriving
the LP, the SDP, and the key-rate formulas.}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
% Paragraph 4: the where_key distinction and key-rate bookkeeping.
% ------------------------------------------------------------------
A structural distinction that our framework makes explicit is
\emph{which rounds generate key}. In the simplest protocols every
preparation setting $x$ and every measurement setting $y$ is used to
establish a key dit, and the key rate per experimental run coincides
with the key rate per key-generating round. In general, however, a
protocol may designate only a subset of preparations as key-generating
for each measurement setting---in our code, a nontrivial \wherekey{}
assignment listing, for each $y$, the values of $x$ whose rounds
contribute to the key---with the remaining rounds spent on parameter
estimation or discarded at sifting. This gives the notion of ``key rate
per experimental run'' a more nuanced meaning: it is the key rate per
key-generating round discounted by the fraction of rounds that are
key-generating at all, and optimizing the choice of \wherekey{} (which
our pipeline can do automatically) trades the quality of each key dit
against the rate at which key rounds occur. \todo{Give a small worked
example contrasting trivial and nontrivial \wherekey{}.}

\subsection{Warm-up: key from a single measurement setting}
\label{sec:results-single-setting}

We begin with the simplest protocol our framework analyzes: one in which
only a \emph{single} measurement setting of Bob is ever used to establish
key. This case is pedagogically privileged. When several settings
generate key, Eve profits from holding a quantum memory: she lets her
probe interact with the system in transit, waits for the public
announcement of Bob's setting $y$, and only then measures her probe with
a $y$-dependent measurement. But when the key is distilled from a single
setting $y^{\ast}$, nothing is gained by waiting: on every
key-generating round the announcement is a foregone conclusion, and
Eve's optimal individual attack collapses to one fixed probe measurement
chosen in advance. The full apparatus of delayed, setting-dependent
product measurements --- introduced in
Sec.~\ref{sec:adversaries} --- is therefore not yet needed here,
and the reader may treat this subsection as a self-contained warm-up.
(Parameter-estimation rounds on the other settings are still essential:
they are what pins down the observed behavior, and with it Eve's
constraints.)

The protocol is a deliberate twist on BB84~\cite{BB84}. Alice prepares,
uniformly at random, one of the four qubit states
$\ket{0},\ket{1},\ket{+},\ket{-}$ --- the BB84 states, whose two basis
ensembles furnish the preparation operational equivalence
$\tfrac12\rho_{0}+\tfrac12\rho_{1}=\tfrac12\rho_{+}+\tfrac12\rho_{-}$.
Bob, however, does \emph{not} measure in the $Z$ and $X$ bases. His two
settings are the intermediate bases rotated by $\pi/4$ in the $X$--$Z$
plane (eigenbases at angles $\pi/4$ and $3\pi/4$). The raw key dit for a
round is, on Alice's side, the modal outcome of the announced
measurement for her preparation, and on Bob's side his actual outcome;
the mismatch is paid for in error correction, at cost
$H(K|B,y^{\ast})=h\!\left(\cos^{2}\tfrac{\pi}{8}\right)\approx 0.6009$
bits per key round, where $h$ is the binary entropy.

Why not plain BB84? Because against the operational adversary, plain
BB84 --- Alice's four states, Bob measuring $Z$ and $X$ --- certifies
\emph{nothing}. Our LP, which is exact for that adversary, returns
$\pguess = \tfrac34$ for either setting, and the resulting key rate
vanishes ($0.0000$ bits reverse-Fano; the min-entropy bound is strictly
negative).\footnote{Every number in this subsection's text and in
Table~\ref{tab:single-setting} is recomputed and checked, claim by
claim, by \repoScript{verify\_single\_setting\_rates.py}; the
visibility-scan claims and both thresholds by
\repoScript{scan\_single\_setting\_visibility.py}.} No qubit assumption
enters our framework: Eve is free to
realize the observed BB84 behavior and its operational equivalences on a
carrier of any dimension, and in that larger arena the noiseless-BB84
intuition ``no disturbance implies no information'' simply fails. (The
same statistics \emph{do} certify key under an explicit dimension bound
\cite{PawlowskiBrunner2011}; we return to the comparison between
dimension assumptions and operational-equivalence assumptions in
Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion}.) Rotating Bob's measurements away from the
preparation bases restores security: intuitively, the rotated statistics
force any operational decomposition available to Eve to spread its
weight across both of Alice's basis ensembles.

Table~\ref{tab:single-setting} collects the noiseless numbers for the
rotated protocol with key generated only at $y^{\ast}=0$. Against the
operational adversary the LP returns, exactly,
\begin{equation}
  \pguess(y^{\ast}) \;=\; 1-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}\;\approx\;0.6464,
\end{equation}
and with it a positive reverse-Fano key rate of $0.1062$ bits per
key-generating round. Because only half of all rounds are
key-generating (Bob's other setting is pure parameter estimation), the
key rate \emph{per experimental run} is half that, $0.0531$ bits --- our
first encounter with the distinction, developed in
Sec.~\ref{sec:keyrates}, that a nontrivial \wherekey{} assignment drives
a wedge between the two bookkeepings. Against the quantum adversary the
SDP certifies $\pguess(y^{\ast})\le 0.5001$ --- Eve learns essentially
nothing --- so the certified quantum key rate, $0.3989$ bits per key
round, nearly saturates the error-correction-limited maximum
$1-h(\cos^{2}\tfrac{\pi}{8})\approx 0.3991$.

The LP also hands us its dual certificate. For the key-generating
setting the (L2-refined) witness inequality reads
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:single-setting-witness}
  \pguess(y^{\ast}=0)\;\le\;\frac12
  +\frac14\Bigl[P(0|0,1)+P(1|1,1)+P(1|2,1)+P(0|3,1)\Bigr],
\end{equation}
valid for every behavior satisfying the protocol's operational
equivalences; it is tight at the observed data, where each bracketed
term equals $\sin^{2}\tfrac{\pi}{8}$. The witness exposes the security
mechanism at a glance: Eve's ability to guess the key generated at
$y=0$ is controlled entirely by how strongly the data at the
\emph{other} setting deviates from the outcome-deterministic extremes
--- the parameter-estimation setting polices the key-generating one.

Figure~\ref{fig:single-setting-noise} traces both key rates as the
preparations are depolarized with visibility $\eta$ (each state mixed
with the maximally mixed state, which preserves the preparation
operational equivalence). Here the operational-adversary analysis is
fully analytic: the LP optimum is affine in the visibility,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:single-setting-analytic}
  \pguess(\eta) = 1-\frac{\sqrt2}{4}\,\eta ,
  \qquad
  r_{\mathrm{LP}}(\eta)
  = \frac{\eta}{\sqrt2}
  - h\!\Bigl(\tfrac12+\tfrac{\eta}{2\sqrt2}\Bigr),
\end{equation}
where the rate uses the reverse-Fano bound, which for a binary key and
$\pguess\ge\tfrac12$ reads simply $H_{\Eve}\ge 2(1-\pguess)$ (both
identities are checked pointwise by the linked scan script). The rate
crosses zero at the unique root $\eta^{\ast}\approx 0.932$ of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:single-setting-analytic}. The quantum-adversary curve is
computed at level~2 of the Moroder hierarchy (level~1 is no stronger
than the LP away from $\eta=1$; higher levels only tighten the
certificate) and turns positive already at $\eta\approx0.922$; being a
relaxation, it is a certified \emph{lower} bound on the true quantum key
rate everywhere, whereas the LP curve is exact for the operational
adversary --- an asymmetry we dwell on in
Sec.~\ref{sec:results-porac}.

\subsection{Separating quantum from operational adversaries: the
$(3,2)$-PORAC}
\label{sec:results-porac}

Our two adversary classes are not equally powerful, and the
$(3,2)$-parity-oblivious random access code exhibits the starkest
possible separation: \emph{zero} key against the operational adversary,
but a healthy certified rate against the quantum adversary.

In the $(3,2)$-PORAC, Alice receives a uniformly random three-bit
string $x\in\{0,1\}^{3}$ and sends one system; Bob receives
$y\in\{1,2,3\}$ and tries to output the $y$-th bit of $x$
($X{=}8$, $Y{=}3$, binary outcomes). Parity obliviousness --- the
promise that the transmitted system carries no information about any
parity of two or more bits of $x$ --- supplies four preparation
operational equivalences, and the raw key dit for a key round is simply
the addressed bit $x_{y}$ on Alice's side and Bob's decoded bit on his.
The optimal quantum strategy decodes with probability
$\tfrac12(1+\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3})\approx 0.789$, beating the
parity-oblivious classical maximum of $3/4$
\cite{ChaturvediFarkasWright2021}; the corresponding
error-correction cost is $H(K|B,y)\approx 0.744$ bits per round.

Against the operational adversary the protocol is dead on
arrival.\footnote{The noiseless claims of this subsection are checked
by \repoScript{verify\_porac\_separation.py}; the visibility-scan
claims, the analytic LP forms, and the quantum threshold by
\repoScript{scan\_porac\_visibility.py}.} The
LP --- which, we stress again, computes Eve's optimal guessing
probability \emph{exactly} for this adversary class --- returns, for
every setting,
\begin{equation}
  \pguess(y) \;=\; 1-\frac{1}{2\sqrt3}\;\approx\;0.7113 ,
\end{equation}
whose reverse-Fano uncertainty falls short of the error-correction
cost: the key rate is $-0.167$ bits per key round, i.e.\ no key. This
is a theorem about the protocol, not a limitation of our tooling: some
operational (post-quantum) side channel consistent with the observed
PORAC behavior and its parity-obliviousness promise genuinely lets Eve
guess the addressed bit that often.

The quantum adversary, by contrast, is essentially blind here. Already
at level~1 of the Moroder hierarchy (moment words
$\{\openone,B,E,BE\}$, with the U-only Naimark generator reduction of
Sec.~\ref{sec:sdp}), the SDP certifies
\begin{equation}
  \pguess \;\le\; 0.5002 ,
\end{equation}
so a quantum Eve constrained to reproduce the ideal PORAC behavior can
do essentially no better than a coin flip, and the certified key rate is
$0.256$ bits per key-generating round ($0.255$ under the more
conservative min-entropy accounting). This is the framework's headline
separation: \emph{which theory Eve is allowed to exploit changes the
verdict on the same protocol from insecure to secure.}

This protocol was introduced, and its security against a quantum
adversary first established, by Chaturvedi, Farkas and
Wright~\cite{ChaturvediFarkasWright2021}, and the comparison with their
analysis is instructive because the two treatments differ in three
independent ways. \emph{(i) Adversary model.} Their eavesdropper holds
a share of Alice's (possibly entangled) preparation but is assumed to
have no quantum memory: she measures a fixed, setting-independent POVM
in every round, producing her guess before Bob's setting $y$ is
announced. Our Eve is strictly stronger --- she keeps her probe
coherent until the announcement and then applies a $y$-dependent
measurement. \emph{(ii) Conditioning.} Their key-rate bound
$r \ge -\log_2 S_{\Eve} - h(S_{\Bob})$ is parameterized by a single
scalar, Bob's PORAC success probability $S_{\Bob}$, with Eve's success
$S_{\Eve}$ bounded via their projection-based relaxation
$Q^{\Pi}_{1+BE}$ (the same ``$1{+}$products'' level our default
corresponds to, but with preparations and measurements restricted to
pure states and projectors); we instead condition Eve on the full
behavior $P(b|x,y)$, without purity or projectivity assumptions.
\emph{(iii) Uncertainty conversion.} They use the min-entropy
$-\log_2\pguess$; our headline rates use the tighter reverse-Fano
bound. At the noiseless point the three differences wash out: their
bound approaches $1-h(S_{\Bob}^{\mathrm{opt}})\approx0.256$ bits as
$S_{\Eve}\to\tfrac12$, in agreement with our $0.256$ ($0.2557$
min-entropy). Away from it they trade off. Under uniform
depolarization ($S_{\Bob}(\eta)=\tfrac12+\tfrac{\eta}{2\sqrt3}$), their
certificate turns on at $S_{\Bob}\approx0.776$; ours at
$S_{\Bob}\approx0.7805$ under the same min-entropy conversion ---
slightly later, the price of granting Eve memory and adaptivity ---
but at $S_{\Bob}\approx0.7725$, slightly \emph{earlier}, once the
reverse-Fano conversion and full-behavior conditioning are used. The
net effect is that the stronger adversary model costs essentially
nothing in the certified noise window.

The asymmetry between the two solvers deserves emphasis, because the
two numbers above have different epistemic standings. The LP value is
\emph{tight}: it is the exact optimum of Eve's guessing probability
over all operational attacks, so LP-derived key rates are exact for
that adversary, and an LP verdict of ``zero key'' is final. The SDP
value is an \emph{upper bound} on the quantum Eve's guessing
probability, obtained from a level of an approximation hierarchy in the
spirit of Refs.~\cite{NPA2008,ChaturvediFarkasWright2021}: it can only
overestimate Eve, so the key rates it yields are certified lower bounds
that can improve (never worsen) as the hierarchy level increases, and a
``zero key'' verdict from the SDP alone is inconclusive. Every quantum
key-rate curve in this paper is to be read with that one-sidedness in
mind; conversely, since every quantum attack is an operational attack,
the LP value always caps the SDP value, and our implementation reports
the minimum of the two.

Figure~\ref{fig:porac-noise} traces both adversaries under
depolarization of Alice's preparations with visibility $\eta$ (which
preserves parity obliviousness). As in
Sec.~\ref{sec:results-single-setting}, the operational-adversary
analysis is fully analytic:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:porac-analytic}
  \pguess(\eta)=1-\frac{\eta}{2\sqrt3},
  \qquad
  r_{\mathrm{LP}}(\eta)=\frac{\eta}{\sqrt3}
  - h\!\Bigl(\tfrac12+\tfrac{\eta}{2\sqrt3}\Bigr),
\end{equation}
and $r_{\mathrm{LP}}(\eta)<0$ for every $\eta\in[0,1]$: the separation
is not an artifact of the noiseless point --- this protocol certifies
no key against post-quantum side channels at \emph{any} noise level.
The certified quantum rate remains positive down to
$\eta^{\ast}\approx 0.944$.

\subsection{Designing the key map: nontrivial \wherekey{}}
\label{sec:results-wherekey}

In the protocols of the previous subsections every preparation was
key-generating for every key setting. In general a protocol may declare
only a subset of preparations key-generating for each setting --- the
\wherekey{} assignment of Sec.~\ref{sec:keyrates} --- spending the
remaining $(x,y)$ pairs on parameter estimation. This is a genuine
design dimension: restricting \wherekey{} lowers the error-correction
cost per key dit (only well-correlated pairs remain) and typically also
lowers Eve's conditional guessing advantage, at the price of a smaller
fraction of key-generating rounds. The correct figure of merit for the
trade is the key rate \emph{per experimental run}: the per-key-run rate
discounted by the key-generation probability. Our pipeline optimizes
\wherekey{} automatically by sweeping thresholded families of
assignments and keeping the per-experimental-run optimum
(Sec.~\ref{sec:keyrates}).

Table~\ref{tab:wherekey} shows the effect across our protocol family,
against the operational adversary (LP, exact).\footnote{All rates and
optimizer outputs quoted in this subsection are checked by
\repoScript{verify\_where\_key\_table.py}.} The improvements are far
from marginal. For the hexagon protocol (six preparations on the
$X$--$Z$ great circle, three antipodal binary measurements) the trivial
assignment yields a nearly worthless $0.017$ bits per run: each
measurement is well correlated with only its own antipodal preparation
pair, and averaging the honest parties' error-correction cost over all
six preparations almost erases the key. The optimizer discovers exactly
the aligned assignment --- each setting keys only on its own antipodal
pair --- and the rate per experimental run improves tenfold to
$0.167$ bits, even though only one round in three now generates key.
The Cabello 18-ray Kochen--Specker protocol is more dramatic still:
under the trivial assignment the rate is \emph{negative} (no key at
all), while keying each of the nine contexts on its own four rays
yields $0.75$ bits per key round, $0.167$ per experimental run. A
protocol that appears broken can be a well-designed \wherekey{} away
from working.

Two structural lessons emerge. First, the optimum is often the
``aligned'' assignment in which each measurement keys only on the
preparations it can decode near-deterministically --- but not always:
in the $(16,8)$ ring the optimizer retains ten of sixteen preparations
per setting, accepting imperfect correlation in exchange for key-round
frequency. Second, the rotated-BB84 protocol of
Sec.~\ref{sec:results-single-setting} shows the optimizer leaving a
symmetric protocol untouched: when every $(x,y)$ pair is equally good,
restriction only costs rounds. The \wherekey{} distinction is thus not
bookkeeping pedantry; it is where protocol design happens in this
framework.

\subsection{Which operational equivalences power the key?}
\label{sec:results-opeqs}

Our pipeline learns \emph{all} operational equivalences among Bob's
effects by linear algebra. But not all of them are created equal. Every
scenario satisfies the $Y{-}1$ \emph{completeness} relations
$\sum_{b}e_{b|y}=\sum_{b}e_{b|y'}$ --- the statement that every setting
has the same unit measurement trace. In the eavesdropping LP these
relations play exactly the role no-signalling plays in Bell-based key
distribution: they force each branch of Eve's decomposition to assign
every measurement the same total weight, which is the entire source of
Eve's ignorance in nonlocality-based analyses against a
general-probabilistic adversary. Any \emph{further} identities among
the effects --- the same effect appearing in two different contexts, a
Kochen--Specker orthogonality closing a loop --- are promises with no
Bell-scenario counterpart. It is natural to ask how much of the key
they are responsible for.

To isolate them, we rerun the analysis on a derived scenario that keeps
the behavior and all preparation equivalences but \emph{forgets} every
measurement equivalence beyond completeness. Since this only removes
constraints from Eve's LP, her guessing probability can only rise;
the question is by how much. Table~\ref{tab:opeqs} shows the outcome
across our protocol family.\footnote{All equivalence counts, guessing
probabilities, and rates quoted in this subsection are checked by
\repoScript{verify\_opeq\_restriction\_table.py}.}

The table splits the family in two. For every qubit protocol the
restriction changes \emph{nothing}: in the two-setting scenarios the
learned set already contains nothing beyond completeness ($Y{-}1=1$),
and --- more interestingly --- in the hexagon and ring scenarios the
genuinely nontrivial identities that do exist (the learned set is
strictly larger than the completeness set) turn out to carry no
additional constraint on Eve's optimal attack. There, all
measurement-side security flows through the unit measurement trace,
just as in nonlocality-based QKD, and the protocols' power over their
Bell-scenario analogues comes entirely from the preparation
equivalences. The Cabello 18-ray Kochen--Specker protocol behaves
differently: eighteen of its twenty-six measurement equivalences are
genuine ray-sharing identities between contexts, and forgetting them
lifts Eve's guessing probability from $0.625$ to $0.671$, cutting the
key rate per key round from $0.750$ to $0.658$ ($0.167\to0.146$ per
experimental run). Kochen--Specker structure is thus \emph{load-bearing}
for the key --- though notably not \emph{essential} here, since the
completeness-only analysis still certifies a healthy positive rate.

\subsection{Key from a noncontextuality-inequality violation alone}
\label{sec:results-witness}

Everything so far conditioned Eve on the \emph{entire} observed
behavior $P(b|x,y)$. An experimentalist would much rather report a
single number --- the violation of one noncontextuality inequality ---
and read the key rate off a curve, exactly as device-independent QKD
reads its rate off the CHSH value. Our framework supports this
directly: in both solvers we may replace the full data-consistency
constraints by (i) normalization and (ii) the single linear constraint
that Eve's effective behavior violates a chosen witness at least as
much as the observed data does. Since this only removes constraints,
the resulting rate is a valid --- generally weaker --- lower bound on
the full-data rate, and it is a monotone function of the violation
alone.

Figure~\ref{fig:witness-only} shows the resulting rate-vs-violation
curves, using each protocol's contextual-fraction witness (the
L2-refined dual of Sec.~\ref{sec:scenarios}, precisely the
noncontextuality inequality an experiment would quote).\footnote{Every
claim of this subsection --- both affine laws, all curve values, the
quantum threshold, and the dual-witness self-consistency check --- is
verified by \repoScript{verify\_witness\_only\_rates.py}.} Three
lessons, one per curve:

\emph{The hexagon loses nothing.} Against the operational adversary,
Eve's guessing probability under the hexagon witness constraint is
exactly affine in the imposed violation, $\pguess = 1-w/4$ (with the
witness normalized so the noncontextual bound is $0$ and the observed,
maximally contextual behavior reaches $w_{\mathrm{obs}}=1$), so the
rate climbs linearly to $0.500$ bits per key round at the observed
violation --- the \emph{full} rate of Table~\ref{tab:wherekey}. For
this protocol a single inequality is exactly as powerful as full
tomography of the behavior, against every operational (hence every
quantum) eavesdropper.

\emph{The rotated BB84 needs the quantum assumption.} Its
contextual-fraction witness constrains the operational Eve only as
$\pguess = 1-w/2$, i.e.\ a rate of $w - h(\cos^{2}\tfrac{\pi}{8})$,
which at the observed violation $w_{\mathrm{obs}}=\sqrt2-1$ gives
$\pguess=(3-\sqrt2)/2\approx0.793$ --- short of certifying key
($-0.187$ bits): against post-quantum side channels, this protocol's
violation alone is not enough, even though its full behavior is
(Sec.~\ref{sec:results-single-setting}). The quantum adversary is
another story: at level~2 of the Moroder hierarchy the maximal
violation pins a quantum Eve to $\pguess\le 0.5001$, recovering the
full-data quantum rate of $0.399$ bits, and the certified rate stays
positive for violations above ${\approx}\,76\%$ of maximal --- a
genuine one-parameter security curve in the style of rate-vs-CHSH
plots in device-independent QKD.

\emph{The right inequality always exists.} The contextual-fraction
witness is a natural choice but not the optimal one. The eavesdropping
LP's own dual (Sec.~\ref{sec:lp}) hands us, for each protocol, a
witness with the defining property $\pguess\le\langle c,P\rangle$ for
every behavior obeying the operational equivalences; constraining Eve
by \emph{that} inequality at its observed value reproduces the
full-data rate identically (we verify this self-consistency in the test
suite). So ``key from one inequality'' is always achievable --- the
framework tells the experimentalist which inequality to target.

This subsection's analysis is what makes the framework experimentally
friendly. The idealized operational equivalences a witness presumes
never hold exactly in the lab, but they can always be manufactured
exactly in post-processing by Spekkens-style \emph{secondary
procedures}: small convex mixtures of the performed preparations and
measurements chosen so the promised equivalences hold by construction,
as introduced experimentally in Ref.~\cite{Mazurek2016} and developed,
in contradistinction to cut-to-your-cloth analyses that weaken the
claim to fit the data, in Ref.~\cite{Zhang2025secondary}. Combined
with the curves above, an experiment need only (i) lapidate its raw
data into secondary procedures satisfying the target equivalences and
(ii) evaluate one linear witness on the result.

\subsection{Random access codes: key rate versus dimension and word
count}
\label{sec:results-qrac}

Random access codes are the natural generator of protocols for our
framework. In an $(n,d)$-RAC, Alice receives a uniformly random word
$x\in\{0,\dots,d-1\}^{n}$ and sends a single $d$-level system; Bob
receives $y\in\{1,\dots,n\}$ and tries to output the addressed letter
$x_{y}$. The key function is then canonical --- the raw key dit is
$x_{y}$ itself, with \wherekey{} trivial --- and the QRAC literature
supplies both optimal constructions and closed-form benchmarks: the
optimal quantum success of the $(2,d)$-RAC is
$\tfrac12(1+\tfrac{1}{\sqrt d})$, achieved with two mutually unbiased
bases and beating the classical optimum
$\tfrac12(1+\tfrac1d)$~\cite{TavakoliQRAC2015,AmbainisKravchenko2024,FarkasKaniewski2019}
(Ref.~\cite{AmbainisKravchenko2024} gives closed forms for
$n=2,3,4$ and arbitrary $d$, their Eqs.~(45)--(50); optimality for
$n=2$ is due to Ref.~\cite{FarkasKaniewski2019}, whose self-testing
result also fixes the optimal apparatus to be MUBs). Our rotated-BB84
warm-up of Sec.~\ref{sec:results-single-setting} is precisely the
$(2,2)$ member of this family, and the $(3,2)$-PORAC of
Sec.~\ref{sec:results-porac} its parity-oblivious cousin. It is
tempting to expect the quantum advantage of high-$d$ QRACs to power
correspondingly strong key rates. The truth is more interesting, in
two acts.\footnote{Every number in this subsection is recomputed and
checked by \repoScript{scan\_qrac\_family.py}.}

\emph{Act one: plain QRACs certify nothing.} We built the
proven-optimal $(2,d)$ ensembles for $d=2,\dots,8$ and confirmed Bob's
success $\tfrac12(1+\tfrac1{\sqrt d})$ to machine precision in each
case. Yet for every \emph{odd} $d$ the $d^{2}$ optimal states are
linearly \emph{independent}: the ensemble supports no preparation
operational equivalence whatsoever, so the operational adversary
reproduces each preparation exactly and guesses the key dit with
certainty ($\pguess=1$), and --- by Observation~3 of
Ref.~\cite{ChaturvediFarkasWright2021} --- the behavior is not even
contextual. Even values of $d$ retain a handful of accidental
equivalences (one for $d=2$, four for $d=4$, twelve for $d=8$), but
only $d=2$ yields a positive rate. This is a sharp operational
illustration of the trade-off of Ref.~\cite{Baldijao2026}: contextual
advantage \emph{requires} limited distinguishability, i.e.\ linear
dependence among the preparations, and the unconstrained QRAC optimum
buys Bob's success precisely by making Alice's states as
distinguishable as the task allows. Quantum advantage in the RAC
figure of merit is thus emphatically \emph{not} a proxy for key: what
powers the key is never how well Bob decodes, but which linear
identities Alice's ensemble is promised to satisfy.

\emph{Act two: obliviousness buys the key back, at a price that grows
with $d$.} The $d$-ary oblivious multiplexing task of
Ref.~\cite{Baldijao2026} (Example~1; see also
Refs.~\cite{SpekkensPOM2009,AmbainisBanik2019}) supplies exactly the
missing promises: the encoding must hide every $d$-ary ``parity''
$a\cdot x \bmod d$ with all components of $a$ nonzero, each hidden
direction contributing $d-1$ preparation equivalences. For each
$d$ we optimize the protocol by a convex see-saw (for fixed
measurements, the optimal oblivious ensemble is a single SDP; for
fixed states, each decoding POVM is another) and feed the result to
our pipeline. Table~\ref{tab:qrac} collects the outcome. At $d=2$
obliviousness is free --- the see-saw recovers the Spekkens
parity-oblivious optimum $\tfrac12(1+\tfrac1{\sqrt2})$, which is the
unconstrained QRAC value, and the protocol is the rotated BB84. For
every $d\ge3$ the constraint genuinely bites (MUB decoding is no
longer optimal, consistent with the biased-basis constructions of
Ref.~\cite{AmbainisBanik2019}), Bob's success drops below the
unconstrained optimum, and the certified rates tell the
dimension-versus-security story quantitatively.
